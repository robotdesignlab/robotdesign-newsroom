NASA's Curiosity rover operations at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, California, run on a tightly compressed daily schedule, according to an account from the mission's project scientist. The rover's overnight data — images and measurements collected on Mars and relayed to Earth via a passing Mars orbiter — must be reviewed and converted into new commands within a single morning shift.

By Ashwin R. Vasavada, Curiosity's project scientist, the Rover Operations Center comes alive at 7:30 a.m., with the first arrival finding the night's data already waiting on servers at JPL and partner institutions worldwide. The operational rhythm is dictated by Mars itself: the team works against the clock before the Sun rises on Mars and Curiosity looks toward Earth expecting its next instructions.

Once the rover operations shift begins at 8:15 a.m., a few dozen engineers and scientists have just three hours to check the rover's health, analyze the new science data, and agree on the next set of rover activities. They then spend another four hours turning those plans into rover commands for the following day, verifying that the commands are safe and fit within the rover's available time and energy.

> Once our rover operations shift kicks off at 8:15 a.m., a few dozen engineers and scientists will have just three hours to check the health of the rover, analyze the new science data and agree on the next set of rover activities.

As project scientist, Vasavada describes his role as ensuring that what emerges from this seven-hour rush is a set of measurements that advance the mission's science objectives and keep the mission on track to deliver what the team promised NASA and the public. The account frames the daily planning cycle as a seemingly overwhelming task given everything that must happen in that window.

The Curiosity rover used two cameras to create a selfie in front of Mont Mercou, a tall rock outcrop, according to the article. The piece is credited to The Conversation and dated 21 September 2026.
