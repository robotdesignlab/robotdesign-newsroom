The humanoid robotics industry is being urged to reverse its design approach, moving away from building extraordinary physical platforms first and then searching for tasks for them to perform. Instead, the argument is that the industry needs a design method that works backwards from the workflow, beginning with a task that has economic value and then determining what hardware, sensing capabilities, and form factor are actually needed.

The observation comes from an engineering ecosystem working directly alongside humanoid developers to solve core hardware bottlenecks. It notes that robots have captured our imagination, with humanoids balancing on one leg, doing backflips, folding laundry, and moving smoothly over difficult terrain. These viral examples matter because they show that embodied AI has reached a new technical stage. At the same time, the window on what qualifies as a humanoid robot seems to be shifting, as robot companies add wheels, more sensors, new manipulators, hats, and screens, while taking away eyes and heads from their humanoid robot designs.

> If we want to move physical AI from social media feeds to a sustained commercial level, we must reverse our current approach.

The industry frequently gets caught up in robot-forward thinking, building an extraordinary physical platform first and then scouring industrial sites for a task for it to perform. The proposed alternative is to begin with a task that has economic value, consider whether a robot can carry out that task better than a human can, and then go backwards to find out exactly what hardware, sensing capabilities, and form factor are actually needed. The sensor stack, in this view, is born in the task, not the datasheet.

Successful industrial robot forms follow their functions, according to the argument. Starting by designing a platform will likely end up with a compromised hardware stack, because the specific workflow determines the machine's engineering requirements. The example given is a bipedal humanoid robot asked to carry out two different industrial work processes: one involving inserting a pin on an automotive assembly line and the other involving packing a pallet in a logistics center.
